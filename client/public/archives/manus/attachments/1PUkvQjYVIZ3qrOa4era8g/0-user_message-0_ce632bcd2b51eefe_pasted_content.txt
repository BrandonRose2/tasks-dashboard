Of course. Given the high-risk nature of your situation—a California property management company with retroactively created invoices—the preparation must be meticulous, organized, and strategic. This is not a casual review; it's a defense of your business practices against a state agency with a very specific mandate.

Here is a detailed, step-by-step checklist for how to prepare for the EDD audit.

Phase 1: Immediate Actions & Professional Representation (Do This First)
This phase is about damage control and getting expert guidance before you interact with the auditor.

[ ] 1.1. Engage a Tax Professional: Immediately hire a CPA or tax attorney who specializes in California employment tax and EDD audits. Do not use a generalist. They will be your representative, buffer, and strategist.
[ ] 1.2. Do Not Contact the EDD: Once you have professional representation, direct all communication from the EDD to them. Do not answer questions or provide additional information without your advisor's approval.
[ ] 1.3. Stop All Potentially Problematic Practices:
CEASE creating invoices retroactively. Do not alter, backdate, or generate any new documentation for past work. This will be seen as fraudulent.
CEASE paying contractors in cash. All payments must have a paper trail (check or bank transfer to a business account).
CEASE giving contractors company uniforms, business cards with your company logo, or company email addresses. This blurs the line between employee and contractor.
Phase 2: Document Organization & Internal Review
This is the core of your preparation. The goal is to create a comprehensive, well-organized response package that demonstrates your position, even if it's a weak one. Organize everything by year and then by individual contractor.

[ ] 2.1. Establish a Master Audit File: Create a physical or digital folder system. The top level should be "EDD Audit [Audit Start Year] - [Audit End Year]". Inside, create subfolders for "General Business Records" and a separate folder for each "Contractor" (e.g., "Smith Painting," "ABC Cleaning").

[ ] 2.2. Gather General Business Records: Collect the following for the entire audit period (typically 3-8 years):

[ ] Federal Payroll Returns: Form 941s (quarterly) and Form 940 (annual).
[ ] State Payroll Returns: DE 9 and DE 9C forms (quarterly).
[ ] State Corporate Returns: Form 100 (for corporations) or Form 565 (for partnerships).
[ ] Business Bank Statements: All statements for every business account.
[ ] General Ledger: Your detailed accounting records showing how payments to contractors were coded.
[ ] Copies of all W-2s and 1099-NECs issued.
[ ] 2.3. Create a "Contractor Master List": In a spreadsheet, list every individual or company you paid as a contractor for the audit period. Include their name, address, Tax ID, total amount paid, and the services they performed. This will be your index.

Phase 3: The Contractor-Specific Defense File (For Each Individual/Company)
For every name on your Contractor Master List, you must build a complete file. The EDD will assume they are employees; your documentation is the only thing that can prove otherwise.

[ ] 3.1. The Written Agreement:

[ ] Signed Contract: Locate the original, signed contract for each contractor. If one doesn't exist, this is a major red flag.
[ ] Contract Review: Ensure the contract clearly defines the worker as an "Independent Contractor," outlines a specific project with a flat fee, and states they are responsible for their own tools, insurance, and labor.
[ ] 3.2. Proof of a Separate Business Entity (The "B" Prong Defense):

[ ] Business License: A copy of their city/county business license or Fictitious Name Statement (DBA).
[ ] Professional Trade License: For painters, a C-33 license from the Contractors State License Board (CSLB). For other trades, their relevant license.
[ ] Insurance Certificates:
General Liability Insurance (at least $1M coverage).
Workers' Compensation Insurance (or a Certificate of Self-Insurance).
CRITICAL: Ensure your company is listed as "Additionally Insured" on their policy.
[ ] Proof of Marketing/Public Presence: A screenshot of their website, Yelp/Google Business Profile, or a copy of their business card/ad.
[ ] List of Other Clients: If possible, a list or examples of other clients they serve. This proves they are not economically dependent on you.
[ ] 3.3. The Financial Trail (The "C" Prong Defense):

[ ] Original Invoices: Collect every invoice from the contractor. They must be on their professional letterhead, be itemized, and show their business address and Tax ID.
[ ] Proof of Payment: Match every invoice to a canceled check or bank transfer record. The payment must have been made to the business name/account, not the individual's personal account.
[ ] Expense Records: If they submitted receipts for materials, keep them. This shows they are managing their own business expenses.
[ ] 3.4. Evidence of Lack of Control (The "A" Prong Defense):

[ ] Bid Documentation: If you got multiple bids for a job, keep them all. This shows a competitive bidding process, not a hire.
[ ] Communication Records: Print and organize key emails or texts that demonstrate you discussed the outcome of the project, not the method. For example, "The fence needs to be 6ft tall and white" is good. "Be here at 8 AM, use these specific brushes, and don't take a lunch until noon" is bad.
[ ] No-Control Evidence: A signed statement or notes indicating they used their own tools, set their own schedule, and were not required to attend company meetings.
Phase 4: The Interview Preparation
The auditor will want to interview you and/or your coworker. Do not go into this unprepared.

[ ] 4.1. Prepare with Your Representative: Review all the documents with your CPA/attorney. They will conduct a mock interview with you.
[ ] 4.2. Know Your Story: Be prepared to explain your business model and why you use contractors. Your narrative should be consistent: "Our company manages properties. We hire licensed, insured, independent businesses to perform specialized construction and maintenance tasks on behalf of the property owners we represent."
[ ] 4.3. Answer Only What is Asked: Do not volunteer information. Do not guess. If you don't know the answer to a question, say "I'll have to look that up and get back to you." Refer complex questions to your representative.
[ ] 4.4. Be Professional and Cooperative: The auditor is a person doing their job. Being difficult or defensive will not help. Be polite, organized, and provide the requested documents in a timely manner through your representative.
Phase 5: Post-Audit Actions
[ ] 5.1. Review the EDD's Findings: Once the auditor completes their examination, they will issue a "Notice of Determination." Review this carefully with your representative.
[ ] 5.2. Plan for Appeal: If the assessment is unfavorable—and it very well might be—your representative will guide you through the appeals process. This is a formal legal proceeding.
This checklist is your roadmap. The key is to demonstrate that you had a system in place to treat these workers as independent businesses, even if the "B" prong of the test is a challenge for your industry. The strength of your documentation in the other areas is your best defense.
