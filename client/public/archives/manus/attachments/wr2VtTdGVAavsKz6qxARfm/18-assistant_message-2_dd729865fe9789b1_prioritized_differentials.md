# Hanon — Prioritized Differential Diagnoses

**Patient:** 9-year-3-month-old neutered male Bernedoodle, 38.37 kg  
**Basis:** Longitudinal record, CBC/chemistry/urinalysis/UPC, seven abdominal and two left forelimb radiographs, reported abdominal ultrasound findings, and recorded veterinarian discussion.  
**Interpretive status:** This is a case-synthesis aid, not a diagnosis or formal radiology report. Rankings are qualitative because no contrast CT, original ultrasound cine loops, cytology, histopathology, or cultures were available.

## Working problem representation

An older large-breed dog has a recurrent then progressive episode of profound lethargy, anorexia/hypodipsia, reluctance to rise, and apparent pain. Imaging documents a **large (approximately 9.0 × 7.5 cm), right cranial-to-mid abdominal mass effect** that is hyperechoic on reported ultrasound and contains an irregular fluid component. Bowel is displaced leftward and ventrocaudally without radiographic obstruction, free gas, or large-volume effusion. Organ of origin is unresolved; liver, pancreas, and mesentery were specifically proposed. Laboratory data show a nonspecific inflammatory protein pattern, mild AST/ALP increases, equivocal pancreatic lipase, clinically meaningful hypophosphatemia, and a urinary bleeding/protein pattern with a possible trigonal bladder abnormality. No anemia, thrombocytopenia, marked leukocytosis, azotemia, hyperbilirubinemia, or clear radiographic left forelimb lesion is present.

## A. Differential diagnoses for the right cranial abdominal mass

| Rank | Diagnostic category | Why it fits | Findings that weaken or fail to prove it | Best discriminator |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Primary hepatic mass, especially a massive hepatocellular tumor** (hepatocellular carcinoma most consequential; hepatocellular adenoma/nodular lesion also possible) | Right cranial location; large solitary appearance; reported mixed echogenicity/fluid pocket compatible with necrosis, hemorrhage, or cystic change; mild AST/ALP and cholesterol increases; older dog; lethargy, poor intake, and pain can occur with a large hepatic lesion. A single massive HCC can remain clinically subtle until large. | Ultrasound did not prove continuity with liver; ALT, GGT, bilirubin, hematocrit, and platelets are normal; no reported effusion or active hemorrhage. These normal results do **not** exclude a localized hepatic tumor. | Multiphase contrast CT to determine lobe/vascular origin and resectability, followed by specialist-selected tissue diagnosis or surgical histopathology. |
| **2** | **Mesenteric or omental soft-tissue neoplasm** (sarcoma family, including fibrosarcoma/leiomyosarcoma or other mesenchymal tumor) | Radiographs establish a discrete space-occupying mass but cannot assign organ of origin; mesenteric masses can displace bowel substantially without obstruction and can contain necrotic/cystic areas. The reported ultrasound differential explicitly included mesentery. | No tissue-specific imaging sign; no reported lymphadenopathy or metastasis; smooth margins do not determine benignity. | Contrast CT for compartment of origin and surgical planes; image-guided cytology/biopsy or excisional histopathology. |
| **3** | **Pancreatic-region disease**: pancreatic neoplasm (adenocarcinoma or adenoma) versus focal pancreatitis/phlegmon or pancreatic pseudocyst | Right cranial location and reported pancreas differential; mildly increased PrecisionPSL; pancreatic tumors and pancreatitis can both create a regional mass effect, and imaging may not prove pancreatic continuity. Internal fluid could reflect necrosis or an inflammatory fluid collection. | PrecisionPSL is only mildly/equivocally increased and is nonspecific; no reported surrounding fat inflammation, peritoneal fluid, biliary obstruction, vomiting, or clear pancreatic continuity. Exocrine pancreatic tumors are rare. | Contrast CT plus expert abdominal ultrasound; targeted sampling if safe. Pancreatic lipase testing can support pancreatitis but cannot classify a mass. |
| **4** | **Other hepatic/biliary neoplasia** (cholangiocarcinoma, biliary adenoma/cystadenoma/cystadenocarcinoma, hepatic sarcoma, hemangiosarcoma, metastatic lesion) | A focal hepatic tumor may be solid, mixed, necrotic, or cystic and can produce only mild enzyme abnormalities. Some are clinically silent until large. | No organ-of-origin confirmation, anemia, thrombocytopenia, hemoperitoneum, marked cholestatic pattern, or documented metastasis. Hemangiosarcoma is less supported than a nonhemorrhagic hepatic mass but remains important because of rupture risk. | Multiphase CT, thoracic staging, and histopathology; coagulation assessment before invasive sampling. |
| **5** | **Inflammatory or infectious mass**: hepatic abscess, granuloma, infected necrotic tumor, or less likely mesenteric abscess | The irregular fluid pocket could be pus; pain, anorexia, mild neutrophilia, high globulin/low A:G ratio, and recurrent episodes can accompany focal infection or granulomatous disease. | No documented fever, leukocytosis with left shift, gas in the lesion, lymphadenopathy, peritoneal fat change, or effusion. This makes overt pyogenic abscess less supported but does not exclude a contained infection. | Contrast CT; ultrasound-guided aspirate for cytology plus **aerobic and anaerobic culture** when judged safe. |
| **6** | **Organizing hematoma, focal hemorrhage, fat necrosis, or inflammatory pseudotumor** | A mixed solid-fluid lesion and acute painful deterioration can reflect hemorrhage or organization; non-neoplastic abdominal masses are well documented. | Normal hematocrit and platelets, smooth mass effect, no reported free abdominal fluid, and no known trauma or anticoagulant exposure reduce support for major active bleeding. | Serial PCV/TS, coagulation panel, CT enhancement pattern, and histopathology/cytology as appropriate. |
| **7** | **Benign cystic or proliferative lesion** (hepatic cyst, biliary cystic lesion, pancreatic cyst, encapsulated benign mass) | Smooth margins and fluid component permit a benign lesion; size alone does not predict malignancy. A benign mass can still cause pain and organ displacement. | Marked systemic illness and a predominantly hyperechoic mass with only an irregular fluid pocket are less typical of a simple cyst. | Contrast imaging and tissue/fluid characterization. |
| **8** | **Less likely origins**: splenic, adrenal/retroperitoneal, renal, intestinal, or gallbladder lesion | These organs can occasionally produce atypically positioned masses. | Reported ultrasound considered the right kidney, gallbladder, and GI tract normal; mass was not thought to arise from GI tract; right-sided location and displacement pattern are less typical for spleen. | CT localization and specialist ultrasound review. |

### Overall mass interpretation

The evidence most strongly supports a **true large right cranial abdominal mass requiring rapid anatomic localization and tissue classification**. A **solitary hepatic neoplasm is the leading working family**, but the current studies do not justify naming hepatocellular carcinoma as a diagnosis. Mesenteric/omental sarcoma and pancreatic-region disease remain credible. The fluid pocket is not specific: it may represent **necrosis, hemorrhage, pus, or cystic degeneration**.

## B. Concurrent urinary tract differentials

The urinary findings should be evaluated as a potentially **separate second problem**, not automatically linked to the cranial abdominal mass.

| Rank | Differential | Supporting findings | Limitation / discriminator |
|---:|---|---|---|
| **1 (tie)** | **Inflammatory lower urinary disease / polypoid cystitis, with or without occult bacterial infection** | Bladder-trigone thickening/opacity, 11–20 RBC/hpf, 2+ blood, turbid urine, 3+ protein; inflammation and hemorrhage can inflate UPC. | No bacteria seen and few WBCs do not exclude infection. Obtain a clean cystocentesis sample if safe, urine culture, and targeted repeat ultrasound. |
| **1 (tie)** | **Urothelial carcinoma of the bladder trigone** | Trigone is a clinically important site; hematuria and apparent focal thickening are compatible; Hanon is older. | The ultrasound finding was uncertain and could be artifact or cystitis. Consider urine BRAF testing and specialist-directed cystoscopy/biopsy; avoid assuming diagnosis from ultrasound alone. |
| **3** | **Traumatic/collection-associated hematuria or other benign lower-tract bleeding** | Blood can substantially elevate dipstick protein and UPC in concentrated urine. | Does not explain a reproducible trigonal lesion; repeat a properly collected sample with sediment review. |
| **4** | **Urolithiasis** | Hematuria and protein can accompany stones. | No visible calculi reported on ultrasound or radiographs; radiolucent material is still possible. |
| **5** | **Primary renal protein-losing disease** | UPC 1.3 is abnormal. | Active hematuria can invalidate interpretation; creatinine, BUN, SDMA, and concentrating ability are preserved. Repeat UPC only after postrenal blood/inflammation is resolved or excluded. |

## D. Laboratory abnormalities that may be secondary rather than separate diagnoses

**Hypophosphatemia (1.4 mg/dL)** is clinically meaningful and may reflect reduced intake, intracellular redistribution, urinary loss, or another metabolic process. It can contribute to weakness, but it does not explain the mass. It should be rechecked promptly and managed by the treating team based on symptoms and repeat value.

The **high globulin/low A:G pattern** supports chronic inflammation, antigenic stimulation, or neoplasia but is nonspecific. Mild **AST and ALP increases** can arise from hepatic mass effect, inflammation, medications, or other tissue injury; normal ALT/bilirubin do not rule out a focal liver mass. Mild **mature neutrophilia** is compatible with stress or localized inflammation and is not evidence of sepsis by itself.

## E. Highest-yield diagnostic sequence for the specialist

1. **Immediate stability assessment:** hydration/perfusion, pain, temperature, mucous membranes, abdominal palpation, blood pressure, repeat PCV/TS and phosphorus; repeat focused ultrasound if sudden decline suggests hemorrhage or rupture.
2. **Multiphase contrast CT of the abdomen** to identify organ/lobe of origin, vascular involvement, enhancement of the solid and fluid components, surgical planes, lymph nodes, and occult lesions. Include the bladder and urinary tract.
3. **Thoracic staging imaging** if neoplasia is suspected, ideally before definitive surgery.
4. **Sampling strategy chosen after CT:** cytology and/or core biopsy only if the specialist judges the route safe and representative. Check platelet count and coagulation profile first. If infection is plausible, submit aspirate for cytology and aerobic/anaerobic culture. Some resectable masses may be better diagnosed by surgical excision rather than percutaneous biopsy.
5. **Concurrent urinary workup:** repeat targeted bladder ultrasound, clean urinalysis and culture, then BRAF testing and/or cystoscopy/biopsy if the trigonal lesion persists.
6. **Reassess limb/cervical pain after stabilization:** focused orthopedic and neurologic examination; advanced imaging if deficits, focal pain, or muscle loss persist.

## F. Urgent escalation signs

Seek immediate emergency veterinary reassessment rather than waiting for a routine appointment if Hanon develops **collapse, pale/white gums, rapid or difficult breathing, a distending or suddenly more painful abdomen, repeated vomiting, inability to keep water down, black/tarry stool, inability to urinate, new weakness or disorientation, or rapidly worsening inability to stand**. These signs can indicate hemorrhage, rupture, obstruction, severe dehydration, or another acute complication.

## Bottom line

The leading explanation is a **localized primary hepatic mass**, with **mesenteric/omental soft-tissue neoplasia** and **pancreatic-region neoplastic or inflammatory disease** close behind. Infection, organizing hemorrhage, and benign cystic/proliferative disease remain real alternatives. The current evidence cannot safely distinguish benign from malignant disease or identify the tissue of origin. **Contrast CT followed by a deliberate sampling or surgical plan is the pivotal next step.**
