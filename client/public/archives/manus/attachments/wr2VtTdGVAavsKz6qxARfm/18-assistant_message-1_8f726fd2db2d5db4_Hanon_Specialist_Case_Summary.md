## Executive clinical summary

Hanon has a recurrent, now progressive episode of **profound lethargy, markedly reduced food and water intake, prolonged recumbency, reluctance to rise, and apparent pain**, with left forelimb discomfort and an extended-neck resting posture. Survey imaging demonstrates a **large right cranial-to-mid abdominal mass effect**, approximately **9.0 × 7.5 cm** on the reported ultrasound, with an irregular internal fluid component. The mass displaces bowel to the left and ventrocaudally but does not produce convincing radiographic obstruction, free peritoneal gas, or large-volume effusion. Its organ of origin remains uncertain; the reported leading sites were **right hepatic lobe, pancreas, or mesentery**.

The laboratory profile is concerning but not organ-specific. It includes a mild inflammatory/protein pattern, mild AST and ALP elevation, equivocal pancreatic lipase elevation, and clinically important hypophosphatemia. Renal filtration, hematocrit, platelet count, bilirubin, ALT, and total white blood cell count remain within reference intervals. Urinalysis shows concentrated urine with hematuria and marked dipstick protein; a reported bladder-trigone abnormality raises a potentially separate urinary problem. The current evidence most strongly favors a **localized hepatic mass family**, while mesenteric/omental neoplasia and pancreatic-region disease remain credible. Size and smooth margins cannot distinguish benign from malignant disease; tissue classification is required.[1] [2]

**Interpretive boundary:** Radiographs confirm a space-occupying lesion and its displacement pattern but cannot reliably identify the organ of origin or histologic category. Published canine cases likewise show that hepatic, pancreatic, mesenteric, splenic, body-wall, cystic, inflammatory, and neoplastic lesions can overlap on survey imaging.[1] [3]

### Reported abdominal ultrasound

The attending veterinarian described an **atypical hyperechoic mass measuring approximately 9.0 × 7.5 cm**, centered in the right cranial abdomen and containing an irregular fluid pocket. Proposed origins were the **right liver lobe, pancreas, or mesentery**. The gallbladder, right kidney, and gastrointestinal tract were reportedly normal; the mass was not thought to arise from the gastrointestinal tract, and no abdominal lymphadenopathy was reported. A thickened area or possible opacity at the urinary bladder trigone was also noted, without visible calculi.

The internal fluid is nonspecific and may represent **necrosis, hemorrhage, pus, or cystic degeneration**. Pancreatic-region ultrasound can also misassign neighboring lesions to the pancreas, and focal pancreatitis may mimic a mass.[4]

### Left forelimb radiographs

Two projections show no obvious aggressive bone lesion, displaced fracture, or marked joint degeneration. These views do not exclude shoulder soft-tissue disease, cervical radiculopathy, early marrow disease, or other radiographically occult causes of pain and muscle loss.

### Urinalysis and UPC

Urine was dark yellow and turbid with **USG 1.060, 3+ protein, 2+ blood, 11–20 RBC/hpf, 2+ bilirubin, 0–1 WBC/hpf, and no bacteria seen**. The **UPC was 1.3**, but active hematuria and possible bladder inflammation can increase urine protein and complicate interpretation. Preserved concentrating ability and normal filtration markers make renal failure unlikely. A clean repeat urinalysis, culture, and later repeat UPC are appropriate after postrenal bleeding/inflammation is evaluated. The possible trigonal lesion warrants targeted ultrasound; urine BRAF testing or cystoscopic biopsy may be considered if a lesion persists because urothelial carcinoma can present with hematuria, while cystitis and infection remain important mimics.[7]

|---:|---|---|
| **1** | **Primary hepatic mass** — massive hepatocellular carcinoma is the most consequential possibility; hepatocellular adenoma/nodular lesion also possible | Best fit for right cranial location, solitary large configuration, mixed solid-fluid appearance, and mild AST/ALP/cholesterol changes. Normal ALT and bilirubin do not exclude a focal hepatic tumor. A solitary massive HCC may remain clinically subtle until large and can be surgically treatable depending on lobe and vascular involvement.[5] |
| **2** | **Mesenteric or omental soft-tissue neoplasm** — sarcoma family | Credible because survey imaging shows mass effect without a proven organ connection, and bowel is displaced without obstruction. Necrosis or cystic change can occur. |
| **3** | **Pancreatic-region disease** — pancreatic neoplasm versus focal pancreatitis/phlegmon/pseudocyst | Anatomic location and equivocal PrecisionPSL keep this category in consideration, but neither ultrasound proximity nor serum pancreatic markers are specific.[4] |

## Recommended specialist diagnostic sequence

The pivotal study is a **multiphase contrast CT of the abdomen**, including the urinary tract. The scan should define the organ or lobe of origin, enhancement of solid and fluid components, vascular involvement, surgical planes, lymph nodes, and additional lesions. Cross-sectional imaging is superior to radiographs for origin and may improve localization when ultrasound cannot separate a large mass from adjacent organs.[4] [5]

Before invasive sampling, assess current stability, platelet count, coagulation profile, PCV/TS, and phosphorus. The specialist should determine whether the mass is best approached by fine-needle aspiration, core biopsy, or direct surgical excision. If infection is plausible, fluid/tissue should be submitted for cytology plus aerobic and anaerobic culture. If malignancy is suspected, thoracic staging should precede definitive surgery when feasible.

## Urgent escalation criteria

Hanon should be reassessed emergently rather than waiting for a routine appointment if he develops **collapse, pale or white gums, rapid or difficult breathing, a suddenly distending or more painful abdomen, repeated vomiting, inability to retain water, black/tarry stool, inability to urinate, new confusion, or rapidly worsening inability to stand**. These signs may indicate hemorrhage, rupture, obstruction, severe dehydration, or another acute complication.

## Specialist questions

1. Does CT identify a specific hepatic lobe, pancreatic segment, mesenteric attachment, or other organ of origin?
2. Does the fluid component enhance at its margin, communicate with a duct or vessel, contain gas, or suggest necrosis, hemorrhage, or abscess?
3. Is there vascular invasion or displacement, and is the lesion surgically resectable?
4. Should tissue be obtained percutaneously, laparoscopically, or by open excision, and what is the bleeding/seeding risk?
5. Should aspirated material be submitted for aerobic and anaerobic culture in addition to cytology?
6. Is thoracic staging indicated before biopsy or surgery?
7. Does the bladder trigone finding persist, and should urine BRAF testing or cystoscopy be performed?
8. Does the neck posture and left forelimb atrophy warrant a separate neurologic or shoulder workup now or after stabilization?

## Bottom line

Hanon has a **clinically significant large right cranial abdominal mass with an unresolved tissue of origin**. The leading working category is a **localized hepatic neoplasm**, but mesenteric/omental sarcoma, pancreatic-region disease, inflammatory/infectious mass, hemorrhagic/organizing lesion, and benign cystic disease remain defensible. Current radiographs and laboratory data do not safely determine benignity, malignancy, or resectability. **Multiphase contrast CT followed by a deliberate sampling or surgical plan is the most informative next step.**
